    Part Two: Raccoon Wrangler¶

    So you drew the short straw, eh? Here’s some advice for running a game session.

    Safety¶

    Everyone at the table, including players and you, the Raccoon Wrangler (RW), should feel safe exploring themes, characters, and storylines that may fall outside everyday conversation. RWs and players should discuss which themes or topics will be in the game, which won’t, and which might appear only briefly.

    Everyone at the table must feel comfortable saying “pause” to take a break, “rewind” to revisit a prior situation or decision, and, most importantly, “stop” without fear of judgment if anything in the conversation or game makes them uncomfortable.

    If a "stop" is called, remove that scene from the game and steer the narrative toward a different scenario; don't debate its merits.

    Golden Rules¶

    1. People Matter More Than Games
    2. Fun Matters More Than Rules
    3. Make Player Choices Matter

    Improvisation¶

    First up, this is all improv. Improvisation is simply making something up on the spot. In role-playing games, improvisation allows RWs to create responses to player actions and events in the game narrative that they didn’t anticipate. If you can think on your feet and devise creative solutions, you can keep the game moving and enjoyable even when things don’t go as you planned.

    "As a GM [Raccoon Wrangler] trying to bring actor-style improv to your game, it's your job to say 'yes' whenever a player makes an offer and to encourage the other players to make and accept offers as well."

    — Filamena Young, Unframed: The Art of Improvisation for Game Masters (Engine Publishing, 2014).

    Never question the whole raccoon thing — play it straight. Run with the hijinks the players dream up, and throw zany encounters and odd situations their way. Make your players make lots and lots of important decisions.

    So, when do you roll the dice? As Filamena Young says:

    "There will be times when you'll have to say 'no' or otherwise not accept an offer. … In improv theatre, this is called 'blocking,' and it isn't conducive to advancing the story or improving the show. However, you have something that actors don't have! You have dice. As soon as someone blocks an offer, you have a conflict in the game. … Pick up the dice and find out."

    Never block your players. If something is risky or uncertain, let the dice decide. Otherwise, just let it happen. “Yes, and” is your tool; never say “no”. Let a dice roll decide “no”, then spin the results back into more hijinks and more decisions.

    Complications¶

    When players accept a partial success on a die roll, it’s a success for now, but it comes with a tradeoff: complications. It’s now your turn to shine as the RW. Your players are essentially saying, “Bring it on!” The complications you introduce in response to their partially successful actions raise the stakes and add interesting twists to the narrative.

    You can always default to adding a point of stress to the raccoons when in doubt, but introducing additional obstacles and circumstances into the narrative is more fun. Consider:

    • Add a new threat — a big dog comes sniffing around.
    • Split the party — clunk, the dumpster lid closed.
    • Use up their gear — ugh, the beer bottles shattered when the car bounced over that speed bump.
    • Give them an ongoing condition — the munchies (they really need to find that food!).

    Make your complications fun, odd, and essential. Bring it on!

    Structure¶

    The game session will likely go like this:

    1. The smack wakes up at night in a hollow log, a ditch, or another green spot in a large urban setting, ready for adventure.
    2. Introduce everyone and review their backstories for inspiration. Important: always address your players by their raccoon names.
    3. The smack sets out to find gear and beverages — hijinks.
    4. The smack steals a car — hijinks.
    5. The smack races their rivals — hijinks.

    Place This in an Actual City¶

    Pick a city you all know. Google Maps Street View is now your friend and battle map.

    Find an actual park, riverside area, or wooded section of the city to serve as your adventure's starting point. Then use Street View to identify likely nearby dumpsters and parking lots. Zoom in to spot fancy, racy-looking cars parked in those lots. Find some twisting, isolated stretches of road on Google Maps to stage the race.

    Always tie the action and encounters to your actual city and its local flavor and locations. The authenticity of imagining raccoons digging in real dumpsters and driving cars on real roads makes for enjoyable sessions. “Oh yeah, I see a convenience store three blocks over. They have a big orange dumpster on Street View. Let’s head there. And you’ll have to run across a busy highway. But there’s a clump of trees in the median. Wow. Check out that Corvette in the parking lot.”

    Set the whole adventure during a Spring Break weekend or at a citywide festival or sporting event. That'll provide plenty of potential beer and inebriated tourists for the smack to interact with.

    I guarantee you'll always smile whenever you pass a location from the game in real life.

    Conducting a Race¶

    The fun of the car race lies in the chaos that erupts when driving actions are revealed simultaneously. The players must never coordinate or collaborate on these moves.

    You might randomly determine the opponent’s driving actions or set them manually to maximize chaos and challenge. Throw obstacles in the players’ path and add twists and turns to the racecourse. Go big, go gonzo — jumps, cars ramming each other, a destruction derby, and attackers leaping onto other cars.

    Don't play crashes realistically — add a point of stress to all the raccoons in the car, then describe sideswipes, dented fenders, scratched paint, and flying sparks. Introduce a complication or require outcome rolls, but keep the action and the race moving.

    Use toy cars and draw a racecourse on paper for in-person play, or load a virtual tabletop with car tokens for an online session. Move the car by its length for each acceleration action. Wing it and make it fun!
